CMS-0057-F & Payer Modernization

Mirth Connect and CMS-0057-F: The Engine and the Payer Layer

Plans that run Mirth Connect often ask a fair question: why not build CMS-0057-F on the engine we already have? Here is what each piece does, what the rule asks for on top of message movement, and how the two work together.

Series: CMS-0057-F & Payer Modernization Published: November 23, 2026 For: Payer architects, integration teams & IT leaders

Mirth Connect is one of the most widely used integration engines in healthcare, and for good reason. Cloud Health Office is not a replacement for it. They do different jobs, and a plan meeting CMS-0057-F can use both.

CMS-0057-F is the federal rule that requires Medicare Advantage, Medicaid, CHIP, and some exchange plans to offer FHIR APIs for patient access, provider access, payer-to-payer exchange, and prior authorization by January 1, 2027.

01Two different jobs

Mirth Connect, from NextGen Healthcare, is a general-purpose integration engine. It receives, transforms and routes messages such as HL7 v2, FHIR, X12 and flat files between systems. An integration team builds each channel and the logic inside it, which is exactly what makes it so flexible: it can connect almost anything to almost anything.

Cloud Health Office is a payer application. It sits beside a core administration system such as QNXT, Facets or HealthEdge and provides the CMS-0057-F capabilities as a finished product: the FHIR APIs, Da Vinci prior authorization, the decision clocks and the metrics behind public reporting.

Put simply, the engine moves the data. The payer layer is what the rule asks a plan to offer once the data has arrived.

02What CMS-0057-F asks for beyond moving messages

Moving data out of the core is necessary, and an engine does it well. The rule then asks a plan to stand up and operate a set of payer-facing services:

  • FHIR R4 APIs for Patient Access, Provider Access, Payer-to-Payer and Prior Authorization, conformant to US Core, CARIN Blue Button, Da Vinci PDex and Plan-Net profiles.
  • SMART on FHIR and backend services authorization for apps and provider systems.
  • Member consent for Payer-to-Payer exchange and opt-out for Provider Access.
  • Prior authorization through the Da Vinci CRD, DTR and PAS guides, linked to the plan’s utilization management.
  • Decision timeframes for standard and expedited requests, and the metrics behind annual public reporting.
  • Ongoing conformance testing as the implementation guides change.

03Building it on the engine, or adding a payer layer

A capable integration team can build all of this on top of an engine. It is a real project, though, with FHIR servers, profiles, authorization, workflow and testing to design and maintain. The table shows where the work sits under each approach.

04Running Mirth Connect and Cloud Health Office together

For a plan that already runs Mirth Connect, the natural pattern keeps every existing channel in place:

  • Mirth Connect continues to carry data between the core, clearinghouses, state systems and provider partners, including the extracts and feeds the payer layer needs.
  • Cloud Health Office receives that data, serves the CMS-0057-F APIs to apps, providers and other payers, and runs the prior authorization workflow.
  • The core stays the system of record for claims and membership.

The integration team keeps the tool it knows and the channels it has already built, and spends its time on the plan’s own connections rather than on rebuilding payer APIs. The specific channels and mappings are scoped with each plan during onboarding.

05Which approach fits

Building CMS-0057-F on an engine can make sense for a plan with a large, experienced integration team that wants to own every line of the payer layer and has the time to build and test it. Adding a payer layer makes sense when the deadline is close, the team is already busy, or the plan would rather spend its integration effort on its own connections. In both cases the engine stays useful.
