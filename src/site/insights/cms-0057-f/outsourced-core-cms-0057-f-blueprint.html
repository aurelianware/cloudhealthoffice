CMS-0057-F & payer economics

Paying $30 Million a Year for an Outsourced Core? A Blueprint to Keep About $20 Million of It

For health plans that will not be where they wanted to be on January 1, 2027. A worked scenario, with every assumption shown and a calculator for your own numbers, and the full architecture for meeting CMS-0057-F, running securely on Google Cloud and getting your data ready for AI.

Series: CMS-0057-F & Payer Modernization Published: November 22, 2026 For: Health plan CEOs, CFOs, CIOs and compliance leads

Forty days before the CMS-0057-F API deadline, most health plans are not where they wanted to be. In WEDI’s November 2026 survey, about one payer in ten said it was fully ready, 17% had not started, and 62% said the work was costing more than expected, with none saying less (Healthcare Dive, November 18, 2026). ONC and CMS only opened voluntary end-to-end testing sprints on November 12 (ONC), and those run past January 1.

So many plans will go live with a vendor’s FHIR layer in front of the same manual prior authorization queue, paid for as change orders on an outsourced administration contract that already costs more every year. Compliant on January 1 is not the same as working, and the next obligations will show the difference: proposed drug prior authorization through the same API by October 2027 and richer metrics from 2028 (CMS-0062-P, proposed), and the industry pledge to answer 80% of electronic prior authorization requests in real time.

If that is your plan, you are going to revisit your approach in 2027 anyway. This article argues that the fix for the deadline and the fix for the cost are the same project, shows a worked scenario in which a plan paying about $30 million a year to an outsourced core and administration vendor keeps about $20 million of it, and lays out the blueprint of everything Cloud Health Office runs to get there.

CMS-0057-F is the federal rule that requires Medicare Advantage, Medicaid, CHIP, and some exchange plans to offer FHIR APIs for patient access, provider access, payer-to-payer exchange, and prior authorization by January 1, 2027.

01Where are you today?

The prior authorization decision timeframes in CMS-0057-F (72 hours for expedited requests, 7 calendar days for standard ones) applied from January 1, 2026, and the first public prior authorization metrics were due by March 31, 2026. The API deadline is the last of the dates, and the one most plans are behind on. Pick the starting point that sounds like yours.

02Fix the deadline and the cost together

Most plans will pay for CMS-0057-F as change orders on the contract they already have. That money buys compliance, but it rarely buys anything the plan keeps: the APIs, the data mappings and the prior authorization workflow end up inside the vendor’s platform, priced into the next renewal. (We wrote about that pattern in The Hangover Tax.)

The alternative uses the deadline as the first step of a move the plan controls. The same daily file drop that feeds the CMS-0057-F APIs (claims, members and coverage as CARIN CPCDS files, 834 enrollment, authorizations from X12 278 or a UM export, fee schedules, provider rosters) is also the data a plan needs to run prior authorization, appeals, provider management and eventually claims itself. Every function you move after that reduces the scope, and the price, of the outsourced contract.

Start beside your current core. Prove it on your own claims. Then move one function at a time, up to the core itself, on your schedule.

03The worked scenario: $30 million to about $10 million

Outsourced administration for health plans is usually sold as business process as a service (BPaaS): one per-member-per-month (PMPM) price that bundles the core system, hosting, application management, claims processing and provider service. Those rates are negotiated and rarely published, so the rate below is an assumption, not a market survey. Replace it with the number on your invoice.

The assumptions

  • The plan: a Medicaid managed care plan with 200,000 members (2.4 million member months a year).
  • Today: a BPaaS contract at $12.50 PMPM, covering the core system, hosting, application management, claims processing and provider service. That is $30.0 million a year. If your member call center is under a separate contract, leave it out of both sides.
  • The blueprint: Cloud Health Office licensed per member and run in the plan’s own Google Cloud project, at an assumed $2.25 PMPM for the license and cloud hosting together. This is a modelling assumption, not a price quote; license ranges are shared during discovery.
  • A platform team the plan employs: 10 people (engineers, configuration and EDI analysts) at $160,000 a year fully loaded.
  • The operations the plan runs itself: 35 people for claims examination, provider service and recoveries at $80,000 a year fully loaded. That is lean, and it rests on higher automatic adjudication and on providers checking eligibility, claim status and authorizations through real-time 270/271, 276/277 and the FHIR APIs instead of the phone.

What the scenario leaves out

One-time costs: implementation, data conversion, parallel running, staff hiring and training, and any notice period, minimum or termination fee in your current contract. They are real, they are specific to your contract, and they decide the payback period.

Timing: the run-rate difference is the end state, after the core has moved. It arrives in steps, described in section 7, and the foothold step adds cost before it saves any.

What stays the same: medical spend, pharmacy benefit management, delegated vendors and the plan’s own management overhead.

Why the gap is that large

Three things in the bundled price do not survive the move. The vendor’s margin on hosting and application management goes, because the plan runs the same software in its own cloud with a small team. Change orders go, because the plan can configure benefits, fee schedules, edits and letters itself, and can read the source (Cloud Health Office is source-available under BSL 1.1; production use requires a commercial license). And a share of the manual work goes, because claims that pend for missing data or simple edits are a configuration problem, and phone calls about eligibility and claim status are a real-time EDI and API problem. None of that is automatic, and all of it depends on how well the plan runs its new operation.

05Operating securely on Google Cloud

Each plan gets its own Google Cloud project and its own tenant. Some of the controls are in the code today; others are set up for each plan, because they depend on your organization’s policies and your Google Cloud agreement.

  • In the repository today: the tenant is taken only from a validated token, and conflicting tenant headers are refused. Services prove their Kubernetes identity to get tokens. Workload Identity reaches Cloud Storage with no static keys. Token signing keys can live in Cloud KMS. Deploys from CI are keyless, and logging is PHI-safe.
  • Set up for each plan: organization policies (no public IPs, approved regions, customer-managed encryption keys), a private regional GKE cluster, Cloud Armor or Cloudflare as the only public entry, audit logs in a separate project, backups in a second region, and a signed Google Cloud BAA. A Terraform environment for hosted HIPAA workloads is written and validated in CI; it has not yet been applied for a plan.
  • Compliance programs: a HIPAA security posture and a BAA approach are drafted, and a SOC 2 program is planned. We do not hold a SOC 2 report today.

The detail, layer by layer, is in the Google Cloud reference architecture, and our security posture is on the Trust page. Because the source is available, your security team can read exactly what the software does before anything is deployed.

06Ready for AI that serves members

Most plans cannot use AI for members yet, for a plain reason: the data a model would need (claims, authorizations, eligibility, contacts) is spread across vendor systems the plan does not control. The blueprint fixes that first. Everything sits in your own cloud, as FHIR resources and event streams, under your access controls.

On top of that foundation, the code includes two advisory paths today: suggested fixes for rejected EDI transactions, and an advisory claims examiner for pended claims. Both redact PHI before any model call, and both suggest rather than decide. A named person makes every decision, and suggestions are logged. Neither has been independently validated as autonomous decision-making.

Member-facing uses, such as explaining an EOB, a prior authorization status or a denial in plain language, are design goals, not shipped features. We recommend adding them one governed use case at a time, with an agreed data boundary, an accountable reviewer and a way to measure them. Our AI-ready architecture page explains that approach.

07The path, and when each saving lands

Step 1 · Foothold

The file drop and the CMS-0057-F APIs

Your core keeps running. It sends the extracts it already produces, and the APIs serve your members’ data from your tenant. Shadow adjudication re-runs paid claims and shows where the answers match.

Cost effect: adds a license and a small team, and avoids paying the incumbent to build APIs you would not own.

Step 2 · Domain cutover

One function at a time

Move the functions where you feel the most pain first: prior authorization, appeals and grievances, provider data, EDI, member service. Each move has acceptance criteria, reconciliation and a rollback plan.

Cost effect: the outsourced scope, and what you pay for it, shrinks as each function moves, subject to your contract terms.

Step 3 · Core modernization

Claims, benefits, payment

When shadow adjudication shows Cloud Health Office reaches the same answers on your claims, claims, benefits, pricing and payment move, and the outsourced contract can end.

Cost effect: this is where the full run-rate difference in the scenario lands.

Your current contract decides the timing more than the technology does. Read it for notice periods, minimum volumes, termination fees and data return terms before you plan the dates.

09What the money could do for members

Savings in administration are only worth having if they go somewhere. A plan that stops paying for an outsourced bundle has a choice about where the difference goes, and the most useful place is usually back into members’ care. Some of the ways plans could use it:

  • Care management and community health workers. More care managers and community health workers for members with complex conditions, high emergency room use or unmet social needs.
  • Faster prior authorization. Clinical reviewers freed from fax and phone intake, decisions made at order time through CRD and PAS, and fewer services that need prior authorization at all.
  • Member service that answers the first time. Representatives who see the member’s claims, authorizations, appeals and past calls on one screen, in their own system.
  • Value-based programs. Contracts that reward providers for outcomes, which need clean claims, attribution and timely data the plan controls.

For Medicaid managed care plans this connects to the medical loss ratio. Federal rules expect states to hold plans to a ratio of at least 85%, and the ratio counts claims and activities that improve health care quality, not administration (42 CFR 438.8). A dollar moved from an administration contract into care management or quality improvement may count toward the ratio, depending on how it is classified. Your actuaries and your state contract decide that, not us.

The point of keeping the money is what it buys: more care for the members the plan exists to serve.

10Questions finance and compliance teams ask

Is $20 million a year realistic for our plan?
It is the result of one scenario: 200,000 members, a $12.50 PMPM outsourced contract and the costs listed above. A smaller plan, a lower contract rate or a larger operations team gives a smaller number. Use the calculator with your own figures, and count your one-time and contract-exit costs separately.
Can we meet CMS-0057-F without changing our core system?
Yes, that is what the foothold step is for. The file drop feeds the CMS-0057-F APIs from extracts your core already produces, so the core keeps running. Moving further is your choice, one function at a time.
What if we will miss January 1, 2027?
Talk to your counsel and your regulator first. Then shorten the gap: the APIs can run in a tenant your plan controls, fed by the file drop, while the longer-term decision is made. We do not promise a timeline; a readiness review sizes the work.
Where does our data live?
In a tenant dedicated to your plan, in your own Google Cloud project or operated for you by engagement. The data can be exported as CPCDS files at any time.
Does passing the Inferno kits mean we are compliant?
No. Passing an Inferno test kit is not CMS certification or a legal determination of compliance. It shows the APIs behave as the implementation guides specify on the tested cases.
