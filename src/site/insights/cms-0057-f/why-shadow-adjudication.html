CMS-0057-F & Payer Modernization

Why Run Shadow Adjudication? What a Health Plan Gets

Shadow adjudication runs the claims your core already paid through a second engine and shows where the answers differ. Here is why a plan should care: it rehearses a core modernization with no risk, it turns up payment-integrity findings in dollars, and it checks authorizations against claims.

Series: CMS-0057-F & Payer Modernization Published: November 23, 2026 For: Claims operations, payment integrity, CIOs & COOs

Most plans will not buy a “second claims engine” for its own sake, and they shouldn’t. Shadow adjudication earns its place when it answers questions a plan already pays to answer: will a new core pay our claims the way we expect, where are we paying the wrong amount, and are our authorizations and claims telling the same story?

01What shadow adjudication is

Your core, whether QNXT, Facets, HealthRules or a plan-built system, keeps adjudicating and paying every claim. Each day it exports the claims it decided, along with the members, providers and authorizations behind them. Cloud Health Office loads those files and re-adjudicates each claim against your contracts, fee schedules, benefit designs and authorizations. Then it compares the two answers claim by claim and line by line: allowed amount, paid amount, member share and denial codes.

Nothing a member, a provider or an API sees changes. No payment moves, no accumulator moves and no remittance goes out. The comparison is all the run produces.

02It takes the risk out of modernizing the core

No plan switches off its core because a vendor says the new one works. A parallel run, the same claims through both systems with the results reconciled, is standard practice before any core conversion. The trouble is that a conventional parallel run comes late, after months of configuration, when changing course is expensive.

Shadow adjudication moves that proof to the start. From the first file it measures two things:

  • How much of your volume Cloud Health Office can decide, and how often it agrees with your core when it does.
  • What it is missing for every claim it can’t decide yet, such as no contract rate for a provider, an unmapped plan code or a member who isn’t loaded. That list is the work plan.

Load the missing input, run again, and watch the gap close. When the match rate is where your operations team needs it for a line of business, you have the evidence to move that function, and only that one, on your schedule. You can stop at any point and the core never noticed.

A migration usually asks you to commit before you can see the answer. A shadow run shows you the answer first, on your own claims.

03It is a payment-integrity review, in dollars

Every difference between the two engines is either a configuration gap in Cloud Health Office or a possible error in the core. The second kind is worth money whether or not you ever change systems:

  • Possible overpayments, where the core paid more than your contract or fee schedule supports. A rate table that never picked up this year’s fee schedule is the classic case.
  • Possible underpayments, where the core paid less. These become provider disputes and appeals, and they wear on the network.
  • Configuration errors behind both: a benefit set up wrong, an edit that never fires, a contract loaded against the wrong provider.

The run report totals the claims where the core paid more and the claims where it paid less, in claims and dollars, and breaks the differences down by the stage behind them, denial code, procedure code and billing provider, largest dollars first.

Many plans already pay payment-integrity vendors to find these, often for a share of what is recovered. A shadow run produces the same kind of leads from a different angle: it re-prices every claim against your own configuration rather than sampling for patterns. Every finding is a lead for your team’s review, not a confirmed overpayment.

04It checks authorizations against claims

Prior authorization and claims usually live in different systems: a UM platform decides the authorization, the core pays the claim, and the two are matched by an authorization number. When they disagree, it shows up in two ways:

  • Denied for “no authorization” when one is on file. The provider did everything right and the plan denied anyway. That is an appeal, provider abrasion and, in Medicaid, a complaint the state may see.
  • Paid without a valid authorization for a service that required one. That is a payment the plan may not be able to support when an auditor asks.

Medicaid managed care plans are exposed on both sides. State contracts set authorization requirements and timeframes, the plan’s encounter data shows the state what was paid, and audits test whether payments were supported. Finding these mismatches yourself, before an auditor or a provider does, is cheaper than finding them after.

When your files include the authorization file, a shadow run checks every line in Cloud Health Office’s prior-authorization scope against your own authorizations. Where the claim file carries the authorization number the core matched, that is the one checked. Where it does not, the run matches the way QNXT and Facets do, by member, date of service, procedure and provider, and records which authorization it used. The report lists both kinds of mismatch with claims and dollars, and the discrepancy file names the authorization behind each one and, when you load your prior authorization list with its medical policy version, the policy behind the finding.

What Cloud Health Office checks is set per plan: by default, Medicaid inpatient stays, and, when a plan switches it on, the procedure codes its benefit plans or prior authorization rules flag.

05What your claims team gets, and what Cloud Health Office never does

Every run produces two things:

  • A run report: how many claims were compared and decided, the match rate, the dollars where the core paid more and where it paid less, the authorization mismatches, and what to load next.
  • A discrepancy file (CSV) with one row per differing claim line: claim number, core and Cloud Health Office allowed and paid amounts, the difference, both denial codes, the stage and reason behind Cloud Health Office’s answer, and the authorization number. It opens in any spreadsheet.

That file is a worklist, not a set of instructions. Your claims operations and payment-integrity teams review it, decide which findings are real, and run the approved adjustments through your core’s own mass-adjustment process, the same way they handle findings from any payment-integrity vendor.

Read-only, by design

Cloud Health Office never writes to QNXT, Facets or any other core. Every adjustment changes a provider payment and a remittance, and in Medicaid the encounter data sent to the state, so it belongs with the people accountable for those. A file laid out for a specific core’s adjustment import can be scoped with a plan that wants one.

06What it takes

The core exports files most cores can already produce, and Cloud Health Office loads them each day:

  • claims in CPCDS format, the data set behind the CARIN Blue Button guide, with members and coverage;
  • the provider roster, with network and credentialing status;
  • provider contracts and fee schedules;
  • benefit plan designs, mapped to the core’s plan codes;
  • authorizations from the core or the UM platform.

Expect most claims to be undecided on the first run. That is the point of the first run: it tells you which of those inputs to load next. The files, columns and loaders are listed on the trial page.

07How a shadow pilot runs: historical first, then concurrent

Historical first for quick dollars. Concurrent if you want proof Cloud Health Office can carry your core.

  • Phase 1, a historical batch. Send 6 to 12 months of claims your core already paid, with the authorizations, contracts, fee schedules and benefit designs that were in effect on each date of service. Cloud Health Office applies what was in force on each claim’s date, so a rate that changed mid-year is not a false finding. Because the claims already exist, the payment-integrity and authorization findings come within weeks, not after months of new claims. How far back you can recover an overpayment is set by your provider contracts and state rules, so choose a window inside that limit; the findings report shows the dollars by month.
  • Phase 2, a concurrent run (optional). For 60 to 90 days, your core’s daily file is shadow-run each night. This is the parity proof a core replacement needs: how often Cloud Health Office agrees with your core on new claims, line of business by line of business. It also surfaces a finding the day after the core decided the claim, often before the provider has been paid.

A plan that wants payment-integrity findings only can stop after phase 1. Everything loaded for it carries into phase 2. At the end you have:

  • a payment-integrity report in dollars, possible overpayments and underpayments kept separate;
  • an authorization-mismatch report in dollars;
  • after phase 2, a measured answer to whether Cloud Health Office can carry your core, line of business by line of business, and the list of what it would still need.

Your core keeps paying claims the whole time, in your own tenant, dedicated to your plan.
