Create a 10-12 minute two-host engineering podcast episode titled "The Migration Cost That Wasn't."

Use Alex and Jordan as experienced healthcare platform engineers. Make the discussion natural, analytical, and candid rather than reading the article aloud.

The central story is that Part 9 ended with an honest, explicitly unconfirmed hypothesis: its confirmation run's throughput drop (26.27 claims/sec vs. a 58.55 baseline) was probably a one-time provider-healing migration cost. Part 10 tested that hypothesis at 250,000 claims and found it was wrong. A fresh baseline run, needing no migration at all, still ran at 41.43 claims/sec. The real causes only showed up under profiling: a cold Redis accumulator cache forcing a cross-service HTTP call on every claim's first read, all three adjudication-path services sharing an artificially tight CPU limit (claims-service was throttled 81.3% of its scheduling periods), and an NCCI lookup cache with a 10-minute TTL far shorter than a real run's duration.

Spend real time on the profiling methodology, not just the fixes: checking cgroup cpu.stat directly inside live pods to get hard throttling numbers instead of guessing from node-level CPU graphs, and using per-stage adjudication timing to find where accumulatorRead was spending 192ms of essentially the entire adjudicate phase's cost.

Walk through the four fixes and their validated smoke-test results, in order: PR #961 parallelized provider/member seeding (previously silent, sequential, unbounded); PR #967 warmed the accumulator cache per-member during seeding, dropping accumulatorRead from 192/378ms to 4/9ms; PR #968 raised CPU limits to match the throttling evidence, essentially eliminating claims-service's throttling; PR #969 extended NCCI's cache TTL from 10 minutes to 6 hours, since NCCI/MUE reference data only changes quarterly and explicit updates already bypass the TTL via existing invalidation. A 5,000-claim smoke test climbed from 92.73 to 203.80 claims/sec across these four fixes, before the 250K confirmation run even started.

Then cover the confirmation run's two real rounds honestly. Round 1: 147.12 claims/sec (the tuning worked) but 5 scattered mismatches, all tracing to provider verification timestamps aging past a 7-day staleness threshold and triggering a live NPPES-registry check that always fails for MCC's synthetic, non-real NPIs — fixed by PR #970 refreshing that timestamp on every seeding pass. Round 2: a different cause behind 80 concentrated mismatches and a P99 latency spike to 3,806ms — provider-service, still a single replica, dropping connections under real p56 concurrency, forcing the same live-fallback path through a different door, and exposing a second, genuinely separate bug: the live verification service's "Failed" status silently maps to "not excluded" in the gate's logic. PR #971 fixed the trigger (scaled provider-service to 2 replicas) but explicitly did not patch the status-mapping bug — frame this as an important editorial choice: removing a bug's trigger is not the same as fixing the bug, and the mapping defect is disclosed as open, adjudication-safety logic that needs its own deliberate review.

Round 3 is clean: 30,534/32,500 matched, zero mismatched, 1,966 unsupported — the same bar Part 9 hit at 100K, now met at 2.5x the scale, at 145.04 claims/sec (a 3.5x improvement over this episode's own baseline), with P99 latency back to a normal 831.68ms.

The emotional throughline: a hypothesis honestly labeled unconfirmed is not the same as a result. Part 9 didn't hide its uncertainty, and Part 10's job was to actually resolve it rather than let the guess quietly harden into an assumption. The answer wasn't the comfortable one (things heal themselves and get faster) — it was three specific, fixable, and now-fixed problems that had nothing to do with the migration story at all.

Do not imply a production capacity conclusion. This is local Docker Desktop Kubernetes evidence.

The next jump is 500,000 claims — the first scale where the still-untouched Submit chain (five sequential I/O hops per claim, deliberately out of scope this episode) and the disclosed provider-integrity status-mapping bug both get tested harder than 250K could test them.
